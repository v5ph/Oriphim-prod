Part 2: UI design
The single most important decision

Veil is not a CAE application. It is a document review tool.

A CAE application — Ansys Workbench, Femap, Abaqus/CAE,  is organized around a 3D viewport. Model tree on the left, geometry in the center, properties on the right. It exists to build a model by direct manipulation. Building that is a multi-year effort, it's the axis where incumbents are strongest, and it is not what you do.

Veil's output is an argument. Its input is a specification. The correct interface reference is GitHub's pull-request review, or Linear, or a good document editor with tracked changes — not a simulation GUI. The 3D view exists as a small confirmatory thumbnail ("is the load where I think it is?"), never as the main stage.

This decision saves you roughly two years and is also, I think, correct on the merits: your defensible asset is generated by review and correction of a text specification, so the interface should optimize for review and correction of a text specification.

The design principle everything else follows from

Every field in the run brief carries provenance and confidence.

Three provenance states, visually distinct at a glance:

**Stated** — the user or an uploaded document said this explicitly. Quiet, low-contrast. No review needed.
**Inferred** — Veil derived it from context. High-contrast, flagged, with the inference chain visible on hover or expand: "Fixed constraint at mounting interface — inferred from 'bolted to the bus structure' in your description."
**Defaulted** — pulled from a standard or a convention. Cites the source: "Random vibration profile: GEVS qualification level, 14.1 Grms. Source: GSFC-STD-7000 Table 2.4-3."

An engineer's eye should land on the inferred fields and slide past the stated ones. That's the whole ergonomic goal of the screen. It also concentrates review attention exactly where your formulation risk lives — the place where Veil guessed.

Capture the reason, not just the diff

This is the highest-leverage thing in the entire interface, and it's a small feature.

When someone edits an inferred field, a raw diff tells you what changed. That's a weak training signal. Attach a one-click reason taxonomy to the edit:

Wrong value — right concept, bad number
Wrong assumption — Veil misread the physics
Not applicable — this case doesn't apply to my system
Missing — Veil omitted something required
Overly conservative / not conservative enough

Optional free-text, one line. Most people will skip it; enough won't.

A corpus of labeled corrections is worth many times a corpus of unlabeled diffs, because "Veil systematically misreads bolted joints as fully fixed" is a learnable pattern and "field 47 changed from 0 to 1e9" is not. Build this before your first customer, not your tenth — you cannot retroactively label edits you didn't ask about.

Report design

The tier label is the most prominent element on the page, above the title. VERIFICATION or VALIDATION. Never both, never ambiguous. If it says verification, the limit statement — no reference data was available; this report makes no claim about agreement with reality — appears in the same visual block as the label, not in a footnote. README says Veil never conflates the tiers; the interface is where that promise is either kept or quietly broken.

The three visual workhorses:

Convergence plot — quantity of interest vs. mesh refinement, with the extrapolated value and the discretization error band. This one chart is the thing most customers have likely never produced and cannot currently defend without.
Tornado chart — sensitivity of the conclusion to each free parameter, sorted by magnitude. This is what survives into someone's slide deck. Design it to be screenshotted.
Assumption ledger — every assumption, its provenance, and its effect on the result if wrong. This is the part a reviewer reads and the part nobody currently writes down.

Export as a self-contained PDF with the signature block, plus a machine-readable bundle (the run brief, the input deck, the checks, hashes). The PDF goes in the customer's data package; the bundle is what makes the claim auditable.
